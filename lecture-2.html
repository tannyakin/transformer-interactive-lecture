    Part 1: Why order disappears

    Attention compares every word with every other word. So why can't it tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog"?

    A little history: reading with a finger

    In June 2017, eight researchers at Google published Attention Is All You Need (Vaswani et al.). Before that paper, the best language models were RNNs (recurrent neural networks) and LSTMs (long short-term memory networks). They read a sentence one word at a time, left to right, carrying a "memory" forward, like a person reading with a finger under each word.

    That design had two big problems:

    1. It was slow. Word 50 could not be processed until words 1 to 49 were done. You cannot parallelise a finger moving along a line.
    2. It forgot things. Information from early words had to survive being passed through dozens of steps, and it faded along the way.

    The transformer threw out recurrence completely. Every word looks at every other word directly, all at the same time, using attention. That made training massively parallel on GPUs, which is the single biggest reason we have today's large models.

    But removing recurrence came with a price, and that is where we start.

    The attention formula, one more time

    Here is the formula you built in Lecture 1. Keep it in view, because the whole problem of this part is hiding inside it.

    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q · KT / √dk ) · V
    • Q (queries): what each token is looking for.
    • K (keys): what each token offers to be matched against.
    • V (values): the actual information each token passes along.
    • dk: the size of each key vector. Dividing by √dk keeps the scores from getting too large, which would make softmax too "peaky".
    From the lecture video

    The video's recap shows an attention map from the original 2017 paper. Two attention heads in layer 5 of 6, looking only at the word "its", point strongly to the noun that "its" refers to. The paper calls them "apparently involved in anaphora resolution", which just means working out what a pronoun like "it", "its" or "they" is referring to. Nobody programmed those heads to do that. They learned it on their own, a good reminder of why attention is so powerful: each head can discover its own job.

    Suggested reading from the video: the original paper, Attention Is All You Need. It is short and very readable once you know Lecture 1.

    Why order disappears

    The video uses one sentence throughout: "A cute teddy bear is reading." Picture the query for "teddy bear" drawing a line to the key of every other token: "a", "cute", "is", "reading", ".". Each line is a direct link, and each link produces one score.

    The video's point is simple: direct links lose position information. Try it below.

    Look at what the formula actually does. For each pair of tokens it computes a dot product between a query and a key. A dot product only cares about what the two vectors contain. Nothing in the formula says "this token is 3rd" or "that token is 7th".

    Dog bites man, man bites dog

    Take these two sentences:

    • "dog bites man"
    • "man bites dog"

    Same three words, very different meaning. Without any position information, the token "dog" produces exactly the same query, key and value vectors in both sentences. So the attention scores between "dog" and "man" come out identical, and the model computes the same output for "dog" in both cases, just listed in a different row. Swap the sentence below and watch where the numbers go.

    Now do it by hand, so you can see for yourself that nothing in the calculation ever looks at where a word sits.

    The technical name for this is permutation equivariance: shuffle the input words, and the outputs get shuffled the same way, but nothing else changes. For language, that is a disaster. Order is meaning.

    An RNN never had this problem, because reading one word at a time is itself a sense of order. The transformer gave that up for speed, so it has to put order back some other way. That other way is positional encoding.

    Part 2: Positional encoding

    If attention can't see order, how do we put order back in? Six answers, from the simplest to the one nearly every modern LLM uses.

    Learned positions

    The most direct fix: give every position its own vector, and let the model learn it. Position 0 gets a vector P0, position 1 gets P1, and so on up to some maximum, say position 511. Then the input to the first layer is:

    input for token at position p = word_embedding(token) + Pp

    The position vectors are just more parameters, learned during training like everything else. BERT and GPT-2 both use this approach.

    Strengths: it is simple, and the model can learn whatever position pattern turns out to be useful.

    Weaknesses:

    • Hard maximum length. If you trained with 512 positions, there is no P600. The model has never seen position 600 and has no vector for it. BERT is stuck at 512 tokens for exactly this reason. The video puts it plainly: you need to retrain for longer sequences.
    • Absolute, not relative. The model learns "this is position 17", but language mostly cares about "this word is 2 positions before that word". The phrase "the cat" means the same thing at position 5 or position 500.

    Sinusoidal encoding

    The original transformer paper used a fixed formula instead of learned vectors. No training is needed, and in principle it works for any position.

    PE(pos, 2i) = sin( pos / 100002i / d ) PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos( pos / 100002i / d )

    Every symbol, one at a time:

    SymbolMeaning
    posthe position of the token in the sentence (0, 1, 2, ...)
    dthe size of the embedding vector (dmodel), for example 512
    iwhich pair of dimensions we are filling in (0, 1, 2, ... up to d/2 − 1)
    2ithe even dimension of that pair, which gets a sine
    2i + 1the odd dimension of that pair, which gets a cosine
    100002i/da number that controls how fast this pair "spins"

    The key intuition: a clock with many hands. The second hand of a clock spins fast, the minute hand slower, the hour hand slower still. If you only had the second hand, you could not tell 12:00 from 12:01, because the second hand is in the same place. With all three hands together, every moment in the day has a unique reading.

    Sinusoidal encoding does the same thing. Each pair of dimensions is a hand. The first pairs (small i) spin fast, so they tell neighbouring positions apart. The later pairs (large i) spin very slowly, so they tell positions far apart from each other. Together, every position gets a unique pattern.

    Now compute the hands yourself with d = 4, and use one of them to stamp a word with its position.

    Two pictures: the values, and how alike positions are

    The video shows two heatmaps of sinusoidal encodings, from Kazemnejad's 2019 blog post:

    1. Value of embeddings. Rows are positions, columns are dimensions. On the left side (small i), the colours flip quickly between positive and negative, like fast stripes. On the right side (large i), the colours barely change. That is "fast hands on the left, slow hands on the right".
    2. Similarity between positions. For every pair of positions, take the dot product of their encodings. The result is a bright band along the diagonal that fades as you move away from it. Positions close together look similar; positions far apart look different.

    Both are drawn live below from the formula. Drag d down to 4 to see the tiny version you just computed, and up to 128 to see why real models get a smooth band.

    You can check the second picture yourself with the d = 4 vectors. You only need one more, PE(3).

    A benefit the video highlights: because the values come from a formula, you can extend to any sequence length. Position 10,000 just gets plugged into sin and cos like any other number (type it into the clock above). No retraining is needed to produce the vector.

    A hidden gift: rotation

    The authors chose sine and cosine for a reason. Remember the angle-addition identities from trigonometry:

    sin(a + b) = sin(a)cos(b) + cos(a)sin(b) cos(a + b) = cos(a)cos(b) − sin(a)sin(b)

    Put a = pos and b = k. The encoding for position pos + k can be produced from the encoding for position pos by a rotation that depends only on k, the distance. In other words, the relationship between two positions depends only on how far apart they are. That is exactly the "relative position" idea language needs, and it is why every distance-1 pair in the last example scored the same.

    Keep this word rotation in your head. It comes back later as RoPE, the method nearly every modern LLM uses.

    Weaknesses of sinusoidal encoding:

    1. The position gets added into the word embedding and then mixed. Once the input goes through the Q and K projections, the clean relative-position property gets blurred. The model has to work to untangle "what the word is" from "where the word is".
    2. It does not extrapolate well in practice. In theory it works at any position. In practice, models trained on short sequences still perform badly on much longer ones, because they never learned how to use those unfamiliar patterns.

    The shift to relative position

    In 2018, Shaw, Uszkoreit and Vaswani (one of the original authors) proposed a cleaner idea: stop labelling tokens with absolute positions at the input, and instead tell the attention mechanism directly how far apart each query and key are.

    The video gives the motivation in one line: "Actually, we really care about relative position with the attention layer." So change the attention layer instead of changing the input.

    From here on we use the video's notation:

    • m is the position of the query token (the token doing the looking).
    • n is the position of the key token (the token being looked at).
    • ⟨qm, kn⟩ is just the dot product qm · kn.

    The normal attention weights are:

    softmax( ⟨qm, kn⟩ / √dk )

    The idea from the video: add a bias to the query-key scores.

    softmax( ⟨qm, kn⟩ / √dk + bias(m, n) )

    The bias is a single number added to each score before softmax. A positive bias makes that key more attractive, a negative bias less attractive. If the bias depends only on the distance between m and n, the model gets relative position information directly inside attention.

    This one idea produces T5 bias and ALiBi, which decide what numbers actually go into bias(m, n). RoPE reaches the same goal a different way, by rotation.

    T5 relative bias

    T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer, Google, 2019) uses the simplest version of the relative idea you just met: add a learned number to each attention score, and choose that number by the distance between the two tokens.

    From the lecture videoT5 bias: the bias is learned per head.
    bias(m, n) = βbucket(m − n)
    • m − n is the distance between the query and the key.
    • bucket(m − n) turns that distance into a bucket number (more on that in a moment).
    • β (beta) is a small table of learned numbers, one per bucket. βbucket(m − n) just means "look up the learned number for this bucket".
    • Each attention head has its own table, so different heads can care about distance in different ways.
    • Nothing is added to the input. There are no position vectors at all.

    Buckets. T5 does not learn a separate number for every possible distance. It groups distances into buckets, 32 of them. Small distances each get their own bucket, because being 1 word away and 2 words away is a real difference. Larger distances share buckets on a logarithmic scale, because 90 words away and 95 words away hardly differ. Beyond a maximum distance (128 in T5), everything lands in the last bucket.

    A detailThis staircase is T5's rule for keys on one side of the query, the version its decoder uses. T5's encoder looks both ways, so it splits the 32 buckets into 16 for keys before the query and 16 for keys after.

    Now let's watch one head use its table. The query is at position 3 and looks back at keys 0, 1, 2 and 3, so the distances are 3, 2, 1 and 0. Each is small enough to have its own bucket.

    Strengths: each head learns whatever distance pattern it finds useful, and the input is left clean, with no position vectors mixed into it.

    Weakness: the biases are learned, so distances far beyond what the model saw in training are handled crudely: they all go into the last bucket. It is also a little slower, because the bias lookup has to happen for every pair of tokens.

    ALiBi

    ALiBi, Attention with Linear Biases (Press, Smith and Lewis, 2021), came from a paper with a telling title: "Train Short, Test Long". The goal was a model trained on short sequences that still works on long ones.

    Their idea: forget learning the bias. Just use a fixed straight-line penalty that grows with distance.

    From the lecture videoALiBi: the bias is linear, deterministic, not bounded.
    bias(m, n) = μ × (n − m)
    • μ (mu) is a fixed slope, different for each head. It is not learned.
    • n − m is the key's position minus the query's. ALiBi was designed for decoder models, where a token only looks backward, so the key is never after the query. That makes n − m zero or negative, so the bias is always zero or a penalty.

    What the three words in the video mean:

    • Linear: the penalty grows in a straight line with distance. Twice as far, twice the penalty.
    • Deterministic: it comes from a fixed formula and nothing is learned. Same input, same bias, every time.
    • Not bounded: there is no maximum. Distance 10,000 gets a bigger penalty than distance 1,000. Compare T5, where every large distance gets lumped into one last bucket.

    The slopes. For a model with 8 heads, the slopes form a geometric sequence, each half the one before:

    μ = 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64, 1/128, 1/256

    A head with slope 1/2 punishes distance heavily, so it focuses on nearby words. A head with slope 1/256 barely punishes distance, so it can look far back. The model gets a mix of short-sighted and long-sighted heads for free.

    Now the same worked example as T5: query at position 3, keys at 0 to 3, raw scores [2, 1, 3, 2], and one head with slope μ = 0.5.

    So in this toy example the "learned" T5 biases happened to form a straight line. The difference is that T5 could learn any shape, while ALiBi is always a straight line. ALiBi trades flexibility for simplicity and the ability to handle longer inputs.

    Why ALiBi extrapolates well. A straight-line penalty works the same at distance 5,000 as at distance 50. There is nothing new for the model to meet. Models trained on 1,024 tokens with ALiBi handled 2,048 or more at test time far better than models with sinusoidal encoding.

    Who used it: BLOOM (the 176B open multilingual model) and MPT from MosaicML.

    RoPE

    RoPE, Rotary Position Embedding (Su et al., 2021, introduced in the RoFormer paper), is the method that won. The lecture calls it the "default choice nowadays: rotations in the attention layer." Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma, DeepSeek, PaLM and most modern open LLMs use it.

    From the lecture videoIdea: rotate the query and key vectors with a rotation matrix.

    Remember the "rotation" hint from the sinusoidal section? RoPE uses that idea directly. Instead of adding a position vector to the embedding, it rotates the query and key vectors by an angle that depends on their position.

    The core idea in 2D

    Picture a vector as an arrow on a flat page. Rotating an arrow changes its direction but not its length.

    • A token at position 1 gets its query and key rotated by angle θ.
    • A token at position 2 gets rotated by 2θ.
    • A token at position m gets rotated by m·θ.

    Here is the magic. The dot product of two arrows depends on their lengths and the angle between them. If the query is rotated by m·θ and the key by n·θ, the angle between them changes by (m − n)·θ. The absolute positions m and n disappear. Only the distance survives. Writing R(angle) for "rotate by this angle":

    (R(mθ) · q) · (R(nθ) · k) = q · R((n − m)θ) · k

    The left side uses absolute positions. The right side depends only on the difference between them. That is relative position, achieved through rotation. Try it before we do the arithmetic.

    The rotation formula

    To rotate a 2D vector [x1, x2] by angle α:

    x1_new = x1 · cos(α) − x2 · sin(α) x2_new = x1 · sin(α) + x2 · cos(α)

    Now the worked example. We take q = [1, 0] and k = [1, 0], use θ = 30° per position, and score three pairs.

    From 2D to real models

    Real vectors have many dimensions, say 128 per head. RoPE splits them into 64 pairs and rotates each pair like its own 2D arrow, each at its own speed:

    θi = base−2i / d with base = 10000 originally

    This is the same "clock with many hands" pattern as sinusoidal encoding. Early pairs spin fast and carry fine detail about nearby words. Later pairs spin slowly and carry coarse information about far-away words.

    The full RoPE formula, piece by piece

    Step 1: one 2×2 rotation block per pair of dimensions. For position m and pair i:

    blocki = | cos(m·θi) −sin(m·θi) | | sin(m·θi) cos(m·θi) |

    This is exactly the 2D rotation formula from above, written as a matrix. Multiply a pair [x1, x2] by it and you get the rotated pair.

    Step 2: stack the blocks into one big matrix. In the video's words, "extension to dimension d > 2: rotate every block of dimension 2". The result is block diagonal: rotation blocks on the diagonal and zeros everywhere else. Pair 1 only mixes with itself, pair 2 only with itself, and so on. A vector of size d has d/2 pairs, so there are d/2 blocks. Here it is for a tiny d = 8.

    Step 3: apply it to queries and keys. The token x at position m becomes:

    qm = xm · Wq · Rθ,m kn = xn · Wk · Rθ,n

    Wq and Wk are the usual query and key weight matrices from Lecture 1. RoPE just adds the rotation after them.

    Step 4: the key result from the video.

    qm · knT = xm · Wq · Rθ,(n−m) · WkT · xnT

    Read it slowly. On the left is the attention score between the query at position m and the key at position n. On the right, the only place position appears is Rθ,(n−m), a rotation by the difference n − m. That works because rotating by mθ and then "un-rotating" by nθ is the same as a single rotation by (n − m)θ. The video sums it up as relative distance nicely captured. It is exactly what the arrows showed you.

    Attention weight has a long-term decay

    The video's last RoPE slide shows a curve: the relative upper bound of the attention score plotted against the distance |m − n|. It starts high, drops fast, then keeps sinking slowly with a small wobble.

    In plain words: with RoPE, the largest possible attention score between two tokens tends to shrink as they get further apart. Tokens can still attend to far-away tokens if their content matches strongly, but by default nearby tokens have an easier time getting attention. That matches how language usually works, and it is a behaviour ALiBi had to add on purpose, while RoPE gets it as a side effect of rotation.

    Why does it happen? Each pair of dimensions contributes a term that wobbles like cos(distance × θi). At distance 0 all the pairs agree and add up to a big number. As distance grows, the pairs spin at different speeds, point in different directions and start cancelling each other out. You saw the start of this in the sinusoidal dot products: 1.540, 0.584, 0.010.

    Three details worth knowing

    1. RoPE is applied to queries and keys only, not values. Position only needs to affect who attends to whom, not the content being passed along.
    2. RoPE is applied inside every attention layer, not once at the input. Position information never gets washed out.
    3. It adds no learned parameters.
    q = x · Wq · R k = x · Wk · R v = x · Wv (no rotation)

    Stretching RoPE for long context

    A model trained on 4,096 tokens has never seen the rotation angles for position 50,000. Several tricks extend RoPE:

    • Position interpolation: squeeze the new, longer positions into the old range, so position 8,000 is treated like position 4,000 in a model trained on 4,096.
    • Changing the base: raising the base from 10,000 makes every hand spin more slowly, so longer ranges fit before patterns repeat. Llama 3 uses a base of 500,000, the slower curve you just plotted.
    • NTK-aware scaling and YaRN: smarter versions that stretch the slow hands more than the fast ones, keeping fine detail about nearby words intact.

    This is a big reason modern models can handle 128,000 tokens or more.

    Part 3: Add & Norm

    Stacking dozens of layers used to break training. What keeps a deep transformer stable?

    Where Add & Norm sits

    Attention has done its job. Before its output moves on, two small steps happen, and they happen again after the feedforward network. In the original 2017 design, every transformer block runs: attention, then Add & Norm, then the feedforward network, then Add & Norm again.

    "Add" is the residual connection: the block's input is added back onto the sublayer's output. "Norm" is layer normalization: each token's vector is rescaled to a standard size. These two small steps are what make it possible to stack dozens of layers without training falling apart. The diagram below shows one block, read from bottom to top.

    Add: the residual connection

    Where it comes from. Residual connections were introduced for image models in ResNet (He et al., 2015). Before ResNet, researchers noticed something strange: making a network deeper sometimes made it worse, even on its training data. The extra layers could in principle have learned to do nothing, yet deep networks were simply hard to train.

    The idea. Instead of a layer replacing its input,

    output = F(x)

    the layer adds its result on top of its input:

    output = x + F(x)

    The input gets a free shortcut around the layer. So the layer only has to learn a change to x, a correction, rather than rebuild x from scratch. That turns out to help in two separate ways.

    Why it helps, part 1: the gradient highway

    During training, the gradient (the signal telling each layer how to improve) flows backward through the network, and at every layer it gets multiplied. Through a plain layer it is multiplied by F′(x). If that is a small number like 0.5, then after 10 layers:

    0.510 = 0.00098

    The signal has almost vanished, so the early layers learn next to nothing. This is the vanishing gradient problem. Now take the derivative of the residual version instead:

    d/dx [ x + F(x) ] = 1 + F′(x)

    That "1" is a direct highway. Even if F′(x) is tiny, the gradient can travel back through the shortcut untouched, so every layer, even the very first, gets a usable learning signal. Try it: add layers and shrink F′(x), and compare what reaches the first layer each way.

    Why it helps, part 2: the residual stream

    Here is a helpful way to picture a whole transformer. There is a single stream of information, one vector per token, flowing from the bottom to the top. Each attention layer and each feedforward layer reads from the stream and writes a small update back into it by addition. Nothing gets overwritten; knowledge accumulates.

    Researchers who study how LLMs work on the inside use exactly this "residual stream" picture.

    Now make it concrete with two numbers per token. The worked example below uses one residual connection and asks what the token keeps.

    Norm: layer normalization

    The problem. As vectors pass through many layers and keep getting added to, their values can drift: some get very large, some very small. That makes training unstable. Large values push softmax into extreme outputs, and the optimizer struggles.

    The fix. Layer normalization (Ba, Kiros and Hinton, 2016) rescales each token's vector so its values have mean 0 and standard deviation 1, then lets the model adjust the scale with two learned parameters. For one token's vector x with d values:

    μ = (1/d) · Σ xj (mean) σ² = (1/d) · Σ (xj − μ)² (variance) x̂j = (xj − μ) / √(σ² + ε) (normalize) yj = γj · x̂j + βj (scale and shift)
    SymbolMeaning
    μ (mu)the average of the token's values
    σ² (sigma squared)the variance: how spread out the values are
    ε (epsilon)a tiny number like 0.00001, only there to avoid dividing by zero
    γ (gamma)a learned scale, one per dimension
    β (beta)a learned shift, one per dimension

    Why γ and β? Forcing every vector to exactly mean 0 and spread 1 might throw away useful information. γ and β let the model partly undo the normalization if that helps. (This β has nothing to do with the T5 β in Part 2. Maths just runs out of Greek letters.)

    From the lecture video

    The lecture writes the same formula as LN(x) = γ · x̂ + β, with x̂ = (x − μ) / √(σ² + ε). The slide highlights every "Norm" box in the original architecture: each encoder layer has 2 (after attention and after the FFN), and each decoder layer has 3 (after masked self-attention, after cross-attention and after the FFN). The benefit it names is training stability and convergence. Convergence means the training loss settles down to a good value instead of bouncing around or blowing up.

    Work through one token's vector by hand. Then use the twist to multiply the input by 10, and guess what happens before you see it.

    Why "layer" norm and not "batch" norm?

    Image models often use batch normalization, which computes the mean across many examples in a batch. That works poorly for language, because sentences have different lengths, and at inference time you may be processing a single sentence. Layer norm computes everything within one token's vector, so it does not depend on anything else in the batch.

    RMSNorm: the modern shortcut

    RMSNorm (Root Mean Square Normalization, Zhang and Sennrich, 2019) asks a simple question: do we really need to subtract the mean? Their experiments said mostly no. So RMSNorm skips the mean and the β shift, and divides by the root mean square instead:

    RMS(x) = √( (1/d) · Σ xj² + ε ) yj = γj · xj / RMS(x)

    The values are not centred around zero like layer norm's output, but they are brought to a predictable scale, and RMSNorm is scale invariant too. It is cheaper, because there is one less pass over the data, and at the scale of large models that saving adds up. Llama, Mistral, Gemma, Qwen and T5 all use RMSNorm or something very close to it.

    Post-norm vs pre-norm

    The 2017 paper placed the norm after the addition. This is called post-norm. Almost every model since GPT-2 places the norm before the sublayer, inside the branch. This is pre-norm:

    post-norm: output = LayerNorm( x + F(x) ) pre-norm: output = x + F( LayerNorm(x) )

    Why the switch? Look at the residual path. In post-norm, the shortcut passes through a LayerNorm at every block, so the clean highway gets disturbed layer after layer. In pre-norm, the shortcut x is never touched: it goes straight from bottom to top, and only the branch gets normalized. Flip the diagram and follow the teal line.

    In practice, post-norm models were fragile. They needed a careful learning rate warmup (starting with tiny learning rates and slowly increasing them) or training would diverge. Xiong et al. (2020) showed that pre-norm trains stably, often without warmup. Pre-norm models also add one final norm at the very top, before the output layer, to clean up the stream.

    Post-normPre-norm
    FormulaLN(x + F(x))x + F(LN(x))
    Used byoriginal Transformer, BERTGPT-2, GPT-3, Llama, Mistral, most modern LLMs
    Training stabilityfragile at depth, needs warmupstable
    From the lecture video

    The verdict, in one line: "Nowadays: Pre-Norm + RMSNorm." The lecture calls them Post-LN (norm after the residual connection) and Pre-LN (norm before the residual connection). Modern models combine both upgrades from this part: they put the norm before the sublayer, and they use the cheaper RMSNorm instead of full LayerNorm.

    We now have attention, plus the residual connection and the norm around it. The other sublayer in every block is the feedforward network.

    Part 4: The feedforward network

    Attention mixes information between tokens. What does each token do with what it learned?

    What the FFN is

    After attention, each token passes through a small two-layer neural network, the position-wise feedforward network, or FFN:

    FFN(x) = max(0, x·W1 + b1) · W2 + b2
    PieceMeaning
    xone token's vector, size d_model (512 in the original paper)
    W1, b1first layer: expands the vector to size d_ff (2048, four times bigger)
    max(0, ...)the ReLU activation (Rectified Linear Unit): negative numbers become 0, positives stay
    W2, b2second layer: shrinks it back to size d_model

    What "position-wise" means

    The same FFN, with the same weights, is applied to every token separately. Token 1 goes through it, token 2 goes through it, and they never see each other inside the FFN. That gives the transformer a neat division of labour:

    • Attention mixes information between tokens. It is how "it" works out that it refers to "the cat".
    • The FFN processes information within each token. It takes what the token now knows and thinks about it.

    A common way to say it: attention is the team meeting where everyone shares notes; the FFN is each person going back to their desk to process what they heard.

    Why expand and then shrink?

    Expanding to a bigger space gives the network room to represent many features at once, and the ReLU lets it switch features on and off. Research by Geva et al. (2021) suggests FFN layers behave like key-value memories: rows of W1 act as patterns to detect (the keys), and the matching rows of W2 hold what to write back into the stream when that pattern fires (the values). A lot of the factual knowledge a model has seems to be stored here.

    Here is that idea for the tiny FFN in 4.5. Because the example writes x · W1, each pattern appears there as a column of W1; it is the same weights, just written the other way round.

    Hidden featureKey: pattern it detects (from W1)Value: what it writes back (row of W2)
    feature 1[1, 0][1, 0]
    feature 2[−1, 1][0, 1]
    feature 3[0, −1][1, 1]
    feature 4[2, 1][0.5, −0.5]

    When a token matches a key well, that feature fires and adds its value to the stream. When it does not match, ReLU switches the feature off and it writes nothing. You will watch exactly this happen in the worked example.

    The FFN holds most of the parameters

    Count the weights per layer in the original model, with d_model = 512 and d_ff = 2048:

    attention projections (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O): 4 × 512 × 512 = 1,048,576 FFN (W1 and W2): 2 × 512 × 2048 = 2,097,152

    The FFN has twice as many parameters as attention. Roughly two thirds of a transformer's weights live in the feedforward layers. When people talk about "billions of parameters", most of them are here. Is that just a quirk of 512? Slide and see.

    How the FFN evolved: ReLU, GELU, SwiGLU

    ReLU to GELU. BERT and GPT-2 replaced ReLU with GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit), a smooth curve that behaves like ReLU for large values but lets small negative values through a little instead of cutting them hard to zero. Smoother functions tend to train better. Move the probe into the negatives and compare.

    GELU to SwiGLU. Most modern LLMs use SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020). It adds a gate:

    SwiGLU FFN(x) = ( Swish(x·W) ⊙ (x·V) ) · W2
    • GLU means Gated Linear Unit.
    • Swish is the function x · sigmoid(x), a smooth ReLU-like curve (the dotted one above).
    • ⊙ means multiply element by element.

    There are now two expansions of x. One, through Swish, acts as a gate that decides how much of each feature to let through; the other, x·V, carries the content. It is like having a volume knob per feature instead of an on/off switch.

    Because there are three weight matrices now instead of two, models shrink d_ff to about (8/3) × d_model to keep the parameter count the same, as the last line of the figure works out. Llama, PaLM, Mistral and Qwen all use SwiGLU. It is a small change that consistently improves quality.

    Mixture of Experts

    The FFN is also where the biggest recent scaling trick lives. Instead of one FFN per layer, a MoE layer has many FFNs, called experts (for example 8, or even over 100), plus a small router that picks the best few for each token. Mixtral 8x7B, for example, has 8 experts per layer and sends each token to 2 of them.

    The model has a huge number of total parameters (lots of knowledge), but each token only runs through a small fraction of them (cheap to compute). DeepSeek-V3 and many frontier models use MoE.

    That is the full transformer block: attention, Add & Norm, FFN, Add & Norm. Stack it N times and you have a transformer. The next question is the practical one: what does all of this cost to run?

    Part 5: What attention costs

    Why does doubling the length of a text make attention four times as expensive?

    Counting the work

    In Lecture 1 we looked at compute (FLOPs) and memory for training, including the 6ND rule. Now we look closely at the attention step on its own, because it has a special property.

    For a sequence of n tokens, attention computes a score for every pair of tokens. That is the matrix Q · KT, and it has size:

    n × n

    Every token scores every token, including itself. Here is the work that takes:

    Q · KT: about 2 · n² · d operations scores · V: about 2 · n² · d operations score matrix: n² numbers to store (per head, per layer)

    The part to watch is the n². This is what people mean when they say self-attention is quadratic. Drag the slider and watch the square fill in: each extra token adds a whole new row and a whole new column.

    How fast n² grows

    Numbers on a slider stay small. Real documents do not. Here is the same square at the lengths people actually care about:

    Sequence length nScore matrix entries (n²)Growth from the row above

    Now the memory check. At n = 100,000, with each score stored in 16-bit precision (2 bytes), how much memory does that last row need? Work through it below.

    When does attention become the expensive part?

    Attention is not the only work in a transformer layer. The rest (the Q, K, V and O projections and the FFN) processes each token on its own, so its cost grows linearly with n. Per token, per layer, the two costs are roughly:

    projections + FFN ≈ 24 · d² operations per token attention scores ≈ 4 · n · d operations per token

    The first number does not care how long the text is. The second grows with every token you add, because each token has to score against all n of them. Set them equal to find where they cross:

    4 · n · d = 24 · d² -> n = 6 · d

    So attention's quadratic part dominates once the sequence is longer than about 6 × dmodel. For a model with d = 4,096, that crossover is around 24,000 tokens. Below that, the FFN and projections cost more. Above it, attention takes over, and with today's 128,000-token and million-token context windows, it takes over hard.

    The four walls

    "Attention is expensive" is really four separate problems. It helps to keep them apart, because each fix you meet next attacks a different one.

    1. Compute. The n² operations themselves.

    2. Memory capacity. Storing an n × n matrix per head, per layer.

    3. Memory bandwidth. GPUs have a small amount of very fast memory right beside the compute cores (SRAM) and a large amount of slower memory (HBM, High Bandwidth Memory). Standard attention writes the huge score matrix out to slow memory and reads it back. Often the GPU spends more time moving data than calculating with it.

    4. Inference memory: the KV cache. When generating text, the model stores keys and values for every past token. We come back to this in Part 7.

    FlashAttention: fixing the memory wall without changing the math

    FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) attacks walls 2 and 3. It computes exactly the same result as normal attention, but it works in small tiles that fit in the fast SRAM, uses a clever running version of softmax, and never writes the full n × n matrix to slow memory. Memory use becomes linear in n instead of quadratic, and it runs several times faster.

    Step through both versions below and watch the two meters: one counts the work, the other counts what lands in slow memory.

    The important point: FlashAttention still does n² computation. It makes attention much faster in practice, but it does not change the fundamental scaling. To actually reduce the n², you have to stop computing all the pairs.

    Part 6: Sparse attention

    Does every token really need to look at every other token?

    The idea

    Full attention lets every token look at every other token. But does a word near the end of a long book really need to score its relationship with every single earlier word? Mostly no. Most useful relationships are either nearby or go through a few important tokens.

    Sparse attention means each token attends only to a chosen subset of the other tokens. You pay for the pairs you compute, so fewer pairs means less cost.

    From the lecture video

    The video groups this part and the next one (sharing attention heads) under one heading: attention approximation. Both are ways of doing a bit less than full attention in exchange for big savings.

    Longformer's pattern on the running example

    The video draws the attention grid for "[CLS] A cute teddy bear ....." with queries down the side and keys along the top. A shaded square means "this query is allowed to attend to this key". Pick a query on the left to see what it can reach.

    Two things are happening at once:

    • A diagonal band (local attention). Each token sees itself and its neighbours. This is SWA, Sliding Window Attention.
    • A full first row and first column (global attention). The [CLS] token sees every token, and every token sees [CLS]. Information from anywhere in the document can reach anywhere else in two hops: token to [CLS], then [CLS] to token.

    This is the design from Longformer ("The Long-document Transformer", Beltagy et al., 2020).

    From the lecture video

    "Variations include interleaving local and global attention layers": instead of mixing both inside one layer, some layers are fully global and others are purely local.

    The main patterns

    Longformer mixes two ideas. There are four in common use, and real models combine them:

    1. Sliding window (local) attention. Each token only attends to the w tokens around it (or before it, in a decoder).
    2. Strided (dilated) attention. Each token attends to every k-th token, so it can reach far with few connections.
    3. Global tokens. A few special tokens (such as a [CLS] token or a question) attend to everything, and everything attends to them. They act as hubs that pass information across the whole sequence.
    4. Random attention. Each token also attends to a few random tokens, which (like shortcuts in a social network) keeps any two tokens only a few hops apart.

    Counting pairs

    How much does a window actually save? Build the grid row by row and count.

    Seeing far by stacking layers

    But doesn't a token lose the ability to see far away? Not completely. Stack layers. In layer 1, token 7 sees tokens 6 to 8. In layer 2, token 6 has already absorbed information from token 5, so token 7 indirectly reaches token 5. After L layers with window w, information can travel about L × w positions. This is the same way a stack of small image filters in a vision model ends up seeing the whole picture.

    From the lecture video

    SWA is "similar to the receptive field in computer vision." In a CNN (Convolutional Neural Network), each filter looks at a small 3x3 patch. The next layer looks at a 3x3 patch of the previous layer's outputs, which already summarize 3x3 patches, so it effectively covers 5x5 of the original image, then 7x7, and so on. The area a neuron can "see" is its receptive field, and it grows with depth.

    The video's example is Mistral 7B, which uses a window of 4,096 tokens. With 32 layers, information can in theory travel about:

    32 × 4,096 = 131,072 ≈ 131,000 tokens

    The key models

    The modern compromise is to mix: some layers use full global attention, others use cheap local windows. You get most of the long-range ability at a fraction of the cost.

    Other routes around the quadratic wall

    Sparse attention is not the only approach:

    • Linear attention methods (Linformer, Performer) approximate attention in a way that costs O(n), usually with some loss of quality.
    • State space models such as Mamba drop attention entirely for a recurrent-style design that runs in linear time. Some recent models are hybrids that mix attention layers with these.

    Full attention remains the default because it is the most reliable, but with FlashAttention making it fast and sparse or local layers cutting its cost, long context has become practical.

    Sparse attention cuts the cost of computing scores. But another cost only shows up when a model generates text, and it changed how attention heads themselves are designed.

    Part 7: MHA, MQA and GQA

    When a model writes one token at a time, what eats all the memory, and how do we shrink it?

    Recap: multi-head attention

    In Lecture 1 we met MHA, Multi-Head Attention. The model runs h attention heads side by side, and each head has its own queries, keys and values:

    head 1: Q1, K1, V1 head 2: Q2, K2, V2 ... head h: Qh, Kh, Vh

    Owning a full set of Q, K and V is what lets each head specialise: one can follow grammar, another can work out who "it" refers to, and so on. Keep an eye on the K and V part of that list, though. In a moment it becomes the most expensive thing in the room.

    The generation problem and the KV cache

    A decoder writes text one token at a time. To produce token 101, it needs attention between token 101 and tokens 1 to 100. To produce token 102, it needs tokens 1 to 101.

    Here is the thing worth noticing: the keys and values of tokens 1 to 100 do not change between those two steps. Working them out again every time would be a huge waste. So the model saves them in memory. This saved memory is called the KV cache (Key-Value cache).

    Try it below. Write the sentence one token at a time, then switch the cache off and watch how much work gets repeated.

    The KV cache makes generation fast, but it takes up a lot of memory, and a formula tells you exactly how much:

    KV cache size = 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × tokens × bytes_per_number

    The 2 is there because we store both K and V. Everything else multiplies straight through, so doubling any one factor doubles the cache. Let's put a real model's numbers in.

    That is for one user. Serve 20 users at once and the cache alone needs 40 GB. At long context lengths the KV cache can become larger than the model weights. And every generated token has to read this whole cache from memory, so it slows generation down too. This is the inference bottleneck.

    Look back at the formula and ask which factor you could shrink. Layers and head size shape what the model can learn. Tokens and users are what you are trying to serve. That leaves kv_heads.

    MQA: every query head shares one K and V

    MQA, Multi-Query Attention (Shazeer, 2019), makes a bold move: keep many query heads, but have all of them share one key head and one value head.

    MHA: 32 query heads, 32 key heads, 32 value heads MQA: 32 query heads, 1 key head, 1 value head

    kv_heads falls from 32 to 1, so the KV cache shrinks by a factor of 32:

    2 GB / 32 ≈ 64 MB for 4,096 tokens

    Generation becomes much faster, because there is far less to read for every token. The downside: with only one key and value head, the model loses some of its ability to look at different things in different ways, and quality drops a little. Training can also be less stable. PaLM and Falcon used MQA.

    GQA: the middle ground

    GQA, Grouped-Query Attention (Ainslie et al., 2023), sits between the two. Split the query heads into groups, and give each group its own shared key and value head.

    MHA: 32 query heads, 32 KV heads (every query head has its own) GQA: 32 query heads, 8 KV heads (groups of 4 query heads share one) MQA: 32 query heads, 1 KV head (everyone shares one)

    With 8 KV heads, the cache is 4 times smaller than MHA:

    2 GB / 4 = 512 MB for 4,096 tokens

    The lecture video presents all three as one idea: share key and value heads within groups of queries. Call the number of query heads h and the number of groups G. G is also the number of K and V heads. Slide G and watch one design turn into the others.

    From the lecture video

    With h query heads and G groups:

    SettingNameWhat it means
    G = 1Multi-Query Attention (MQA)one group: all h query heads share one K and one V
    1 < G < hGroup-Query Attention (GQA)G groups, each with its own K and V, shared by h/G query heads
    G = hMulti-Head Attention (MHA)every query head is its own group, with its own K and V

    So MHA and MQA are really the two extremes of GQA. With our numbers, h = 32 and G = 8 gives 32 / 8 = 4 query heads per group. The video says "Group-Query Attention"; the paper calls it "Grouped-Query Attention". Same thing.

    Side by side, and who uses what

    GQA gets quality very close to MHA with memory savings close to MQA. Llama 2 70B, Llama 3, Mistral, Qwen and Gemma all use GQA. It has become the standard.

    Query headsKV headsKV cache (4,096 tokens)Quality

    MLA, Multi-head Latent Attention, used in DeepSeek-V2 and V3, compresses keys and values into a small shared "latent" vector before caching them. That shrinks the cache even further while keeping quality high.

    Part 8: Three model families

    Encoder, decoder or both: which one do you need, and why did decoder-only win?

    First, masked attention properly explained

    The 2017 transformer had two halves: an encoder and a decoder. Researchers soon discovered that you do not always need both, and that produced three families. What separates them is one small detail: the mask. So we start there.

    In Lecture 1 we met masked attention briefly. Here is how it actually works. In a decoder that generates text left to right, a token must not see the tokens after it. Otherwise, during training, the model could simply look at the answer.

    The fix: before softmax, set the scores for all "future" positions to negative infinity. Since e−∞ = 0, those positions get zero attention. Here is the causal mask for 4 tokens, where 0 means allowed and −∞ means blocked.

    Everything above the diagonal is blocked. That is why it is often called an upper-triangular mask. Now let's watch one row of it do its job.

    Why this matters for training speed. With the mask, we can feed a whole sentence in at once and train the model to predict every next token in parallel. Position 0 predicts token 1, position 1 predicts token 2, and so on, all in one pass, and none of them can cheat:

    position 0 sees "The" → predicts "cat" position 1 sees "The cat" → predicts "sat" position 2 sees "The cat sat" → predicts "down"

    This parallel training is a big part of why transformers train so much faster than RNNs, which have to walk through the sentence one step at a time.

    The three families

    Take the encoder alone, the decoder alone, or both together, and you get the three families. Each one is defined by who is allowed to look at whom.     • Uses the relative position bias from Part 2.4 and a simplified layer norm (close to RMSNorm) with pre-norm.
    • Sizes ranged from 60 million to 11 billion parameters.

    Why decoder-only won

    Around 2018 to 2020, all three families were strong. Today, nearly every large general-purpose model is decoder-only. Four reasons:

    1. One simple objective that scales. Next-token prediction works on any text, with no special labelling. The more data and compute you add, the better it gets, very predictably.
    2. In-context learning. GPT-3 (2020) showed that a large enough decoder can do new tasks just from examples in the prompt, with no fine-tuning. That made one model useful for everything.
    3. Generation is universal. If a model can generate text, it can answer, classify, translate or summarize by writing the answer. Encoder-only models cannot generate.
    4. Engineering simplicity. One stack, and the KV cache makes generation efficient.

    Part 9: BERT

    How did one encoder, trained by filling in blanks, change the whole field?

    What BERT is

    BERT stands for Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (Devlin et al., Google, October 2018).

    It took the encoder half of the 2017 transformer, trained it on a huge amount of text to understand language, and showed that this one pretrained model could then be quickly adapted to almost any understanding task. When it came out, it set new records on 11 major language benchmarks, including GLUE (General Language Understanding Evaluation) and SQuAD (Stanford Question Answering Dataset).

    It also changed how the whole field worked. Before BERT, you trained a fresh model for every task. After BERT, the recipe became pretrain once, fine-tune many times: one expensive model learns language in general, and each task only pays for a short, cheap adjustment on top. Google started using it in Search in 2019.

    Why "bidirectional" was the big deal

    Before BERT, pretrained language models mostly read in one direction. GPT-1 (2018) read left to right. ELMo (2018) trained one model left to right and another right to left and glued them together, so neither half ever saw both sides at the same time.

    But understanding a word often needs both sides at once. Try it with the two sentences below. In each one, the word "bank" is only resolved by words that come after it.

    A left-to-right model reading "I went to the bank" has not seen "deposit" yet. BERT's encoder sees the full sentence in every layer.

    The lecture video shows where the word "bidirectional" comes from, using the original transformer diagram:

    • The encoder's self-attention has no mask, so every token attends to tokens on its left and right. That is bidirectional.
    • The decoder's masked self-attention only looks left. The video marks it with a warning sign: not bidirectional.

    BERT keeps only the bidirectional half. That is the whole meaning of the "B" and the "E" in its name.

    From the lecture video: why is it called BERT?

    A bit of fun history. In February 2018, the Allen Institute published ELMo ("Deep contextualized word representations", Peters et al.), a well-known model at the time. In October 2018, Google submitted the BERT paper. Elmo and Bert are both characters from the children's TV show Sesame Street, and the naming was very much on purpose. The trend continued for a while (ERNIE, Big Bird).

    The BERT strategy and its trade-offs

    The idea from the video is simple: keep only encoders, and use the encoded embeddings to make predictions. Feed in "[CLS] A cute teddy bear is reading . [SEP]", run it through the encoder, and take the output vector at the [CLS] position. That "encoded embedding" gets projected to whatever you want to predict.

    The strategy comes in two steps:

    1. Pretraining with proxy tasks: MLM and NSP, explained below. A proxy task is a made-up task that nobody actually needs solved, but that forces the model to learn useful things about language along the way. Its big advantage is that the labels come free from raw text.
    2. Fine-tuning for the end task you actually care about, like sentiment.
    ProsCons
    Fine-tuning does not need a lot of dataNot suited for a range of tasks (for example, text generation)
    Good performanceFine-tuning is a required step

    That second con is worth noticing. A modern decoder-only LLM can often do a task straight from a prompt. BERT always needs a fine-tuning step for each new task.

    The video also lists BERT's best-known variants: RoBERTa, DistilBERT and ALBERT. All three are covered in Part 10.

    BERT's architecture and size

    Put the original 2017 transformer and BERT side by side, as the video does, and BERT is simply the left half: the encoder stack. The decoder stack, cross-attention and the text-generating output layer are all gone.

    BERT describes its size with three numbers, L, H, A:

    • L is the number of layers (encoder blocks stacked).
    • H is the hidden size, the dimension of the embeddings. It is what we have been calling dmodel.
    • A is the number of attention heads running in parallel.

    There are also two choices about the data:

    • Language-specific or multilingual: which languages the model was trained on. For example, bert-base-uncased is English only, while mBERT (multilingual BERT) covers over 100 languages.
    • Cased or uncased: whether the input is converted to lowercase first. An uncased model sees "Lagos" and "lagos" as the same word. A cased model keeps the difference, which matters for things like finding names.

    BERT-Base and BERT-Large are the two original models. The four smaller ones came later (Turc et al., 2019) for devices with little memory. In every BERT model, the FFN inside each layer expands to 4 × H (3,072 for Base), and the maximum input is 512 tokens.

    So where do BERT-Base's 110 million parameters actually sit? Count them, matrix by matrix.

    BERT's input

    BERT's input is built from three embeddings added together:

    input = token embedding + segment embedding + position embedding
    1. Token embedding. The video calls it a "gigantic lookup table" that learns one embedding for each word of the vocabulary. Text is first split with the WordPiece tokenizer.
    2. Position embedding. It "helps the network associate tokens with a position", and the encoding can be "either learnt or fixed with cosines and sines". BERT itself uses learned absolute positions (Part 2.1), which is why it is capped at 512 tokens.
    3. Segment embedding. Marked new! in the video, because the 2017 transformer did not have it. BERT can take two pieces of text at once (segment A and segment B). Every token in segment A shares one embedding, every token in segment B shares another. That is how the model knows which sentence a token belongs to.

    The lecture sums up WordPiece in three bullet points:

    • The tokenizer is trained on a training set beforehand, before BERT itself is trained.
    • Vocabulary size: about 30,000.
    • Great at detecting common particles. Frequent word pieces become their own tokens, so rare words get split into familiar parts. The "##" means "this piece continues the previous word".

    playing→play+##ing
    unbelievable→un+##believ+##able

    Three special tokens make the MLM and NSP tasks possible:

    • [CLS] (classification) is added at the beginning of the input. Its final vector is used as a summary of the whole input for classification tasks.
    • [SEP] (separator) goes between consecutive segments, and another one goes at the end.
    • [MASK] replaces the tokens that are hidden for the MLM task.

    The goal of the encoder-only model, in the video's words: represent the input with features that are (hopefully) useful for language tasks, using the transformer's self-attention, and then use those learned embeddings for classification-oriented tasks.

    Pretraining task 1: Masked Language Modeling

    Here is the problem BERT had to solve. You cannot train a bidirectional model on "predict the next word", because if every token can see every other token, the answer is sitting right there in the input.

    BERT's solution: hide some words and ask the model to fill them in, like a cloze test in school. The recipe:

    1. Pick 15% of the tokens at random.
    2. Of those chosen tokens, 80% are replaced with [MASK], 10% are replaced with a random word, and 10% are left unchanged.
    3. The model must predict the original token at every chosen position.

    Why the 80 / 10 / 10 split?

    The [MASK] token never appears when BERT is later fine-tuned on real tasks. If BERT only ever made predictions at [MASK] positions, it might learn to only "think hard" when it sees [MASK], and give lazy representations for normal words. By sometimes using random words and sometimes leaving words unchanged, BERT can never be sure which tokens are real, so it has to build a good representation for every token. The random replacements are rare enough (1.5% of all tokens) not to damage its understanding of language.

    What MLM buys you

    • The network learns language modeling based on contextual information from both sides.
    • The regularization reflects the probabilistic nature of language. Regularization means anything that stops a model from just memorizing. Here, the random 10% and unchanged 10% make the task a little noisy on purpose, which matches reality: a blank in a sentence usually has several reasonable answers, not one. "The teddy bear is [MASK]" could be "reading", "sleeping" or "cute", and the model learns a spread of probabilities over them.

    What it costs

    The model only learns from the 15% of tokens it predicts. A decoder learns from 100% of its tokens, because every token predicts the next one. This makes MLM less efficient per unit of data, a point ELECTRA later attacked.

    Pretraining task 2: Next Sentence Prediction

    Some tasks, like question answering, need to understand how two sentences relate. So BERT also trained on NSP:

    • 50% of the time, sentence B really is the sentence that followed A in the text. Label: IsNext.
    • 50% of the time, sentence B is a random sentence from elsewhere. Label: NotNext.

    The model predicts the label from the [CLS] vector.

    The video names two benefits of NSP:

    • The network implicitly learns to detect useful contextual information across sentences.
    • It is an easy classification task that does not require any labels. You just take real text: consecutive sentences are "IsNext" automatically, random pairs are "NotNext" automatically. No human has to label anything.

    Keep NSP in mind. RoBERTa later showed it was not actually helping.

    Training data: BooksCorpus (about 800 million words) and English Wikipedia (about 2.5 billion words), about 16 GB of text.

    Fine-tuning BERT

    After pretraining, you adapt BERT to a specific task by adding a tiny layer on top and training the whole thing briefly on labelled data, often just an hour or less on one GPU.

    The goal, as the video puts it, is to leverage the embeddings BERT learned for a "sister" task: a related task that benefits from the same understanding of language. Three tricks help:

    • Use the weights from an already massively pretrained model. Never start from scratch.
    • Freeze the early layers. Freezing means those weights are not updated during fine-tuning. Early layers tend to capture general things (word pieces, basic grammar) that do not need to change, so freezing them saves compute and can reduce overfitting. The video calls it "sometimes a better trade-off between complexity and performance".
    • Great results are possible with minimal labeled data. How minimal depends on how complex the task is and how close it is to the data and objectives BERT was pretrained on.

    The video's two use cases are sequence classification (one label for the whole input, such as sentiment extraction) and token classification (one label per token, such as question answering, where you label which tokens are the start and end of the answer). Here is the first one, followed one sentence all the way through.

    The same pretrained body serves many tasks. Only the small head on top changes, and what it reads from BERT's outputs:

    1. Sentence classification (sentiment, spam, topic): take the final [CLS] vector (768 numbers), pass it through one linear layer, then softmax over the classes.
    2. Token classification (NER): put a linear layer on every token's output vector and predict a label for each: person, location, organization, or nothing.
    3. Question answering (SQuAD style): the input is [CLS] question [SEP] passage [SEP]. The model learns two vectors, a start vector and an end vector. For each passage token, the dot product with the start vector gives a score for "the answer starts here", and likewise for the end. The answer is the span with the best start and end.
    4. Sentence-pair tasks (does sentence B follow from A, are these two questions duplicates): put both sentences in, classify from [CLS].
    5. Embeddings for search. A later development, Sentence-BERT (2019), fine-tuned BERT so that similar sentences produce similar vectors. This is the foundation of semantic search and of the retrieval step in many RAG systems today.

    BERT's limitations

    First, the good side, from the video's takeaways: state of the art results (at the time), a roughly true contextual representation of words ("bank" gets a different vector in "river bank" and "bank account"), and easy adaptation to many classification tasks. It is still widely used in industry for anything related to encoding: search, ranking, classification, deduplication, retrieval for RAG.

    The video then names three limitations:

    1. The context window is limited to 512 tokens, because of the learned absolute positions.
    2. Computationally expensive: a hard sell for low-latency or cost-sensitive applications. 110M parameters is small today, but in 2019 that was heavy for a phone or a high-traffic API.
    3. The training paradigm is complex: MLM plus NSP pretraining, then fine-tuning for every task.

    And there are four other known weaknesses:

    1. Cannot generate text naturally.
    2. Learns from only 15% of tokens per example.
    3. The [MASK] mismatch between pretraining and fine-tuning, reduced but not solved by the 80 / 10 / 10 trick.
    4. Undertrained. As RoBERTa showed, BERT had a lot more to give.

    Limitation 2 leads to DistilBERT: make it smaller and faster. Limitation 7 leads to RoBERTa: train it properly. The video covers both, and so does the next part.

    BERT had clear weak spots: it was heavy for its time, it learned from only 15% of its tokens, and it was undertrained. Each model that came next went after one of them.

    Part 10: After BERT

    What happened when people fixed BERT's weaknesses one at a time?

    RoBERTa: BERT, trained properly

    BERT's success set off a wave of improvements, and each one went after a specific weakness. The first asked the simplest question of all.

    RoBERTa stands for Robustly optimized BERT approach (Liu et al., Facebook AI, 2019). The team wondered: was BERT's design really the limit, or had BERT just not been trained well enough? To find out, they kept exactly the same architecture and changed only the training recipe. The lecture video describes the goal as a "thorough analysis of directions of optimization."

    The video sorts what they changed into two groups: how the model is trained, and what it is trained on.

    Modelling

    1. Removed NSP and the segment encodings. The video's verdict: "~no effect!" They tested it carefully and found that dropping Next Sentence Prediction matched or slightly improved results. Instead, each input was filled with full, continuous sentences up to 512 tokens. (DistilBERT had also removed it.) A task that sounds sensible on paper is not guaranteed to help.

    2. Static to dynamic masking across epochs. Original BERT masked the data once, during preprocessing. For some variety the data was copied 10 times with 10 different masks, so over 40 epochs the model saw each exact mask 4 times. RoBERTa draws a fresh random mask every time a sequence goes in. You will count exactly how much variety that buys just below.

    Data

    1. Richer: 16 GB to 160 GB of pretraining text. Ten times more, adding news articles (CC-News), web text (OpenWebText) and stories.

    2. For longer: bigger batches. BERT trained for 1M steps with a batch size of 256. RoBERTa trained for 500k steps with a batch size of 8k.

    Change 4 looks like RoBERTa trained less: half the steps. But a step is one update of the weights, and the batch size is how many sequences feed into that one update. Multiply the two and you get how much text the model actually read.

    One more change: the tokenizer. RoBERTa switched to a byte-level BPE tokenizer with a 50,000-token vocabulary. BPE means Byte-Pair Encoding, the same family GPT-2 used. Because it is built on raw bytes, any text at all can be encoded without falling back to an "unknown" token.

    Dynamic masking, counted

    "A fresh mask every time" sounds like a small tweak. To see why it matters, count how many different masks a single sentence could have, and how many of them each approach actually uses.

    The result, and the lesson

    The video puts the payoff at about +4% across benchmarks, with the same architecture. Same size, same layers, and RoBERTa beat BERT across GLUE, SQuAD and RACE. It also matched or beat XLNet, a more complicated model released around the same time.

    How you train matters as much as what you build.

    That is one of the most important lessons in modern AI. More data, more training and cleaner objectives gave large gains without changing a single layer. Keep it in mind: it points straight at the scaling era that produced today's LLMs, which is where Part 11 ends up.

    DistilBERT: smaller and faster

    RoBERTa went after "undertrained". DistilBERT (Sanh et al., Hugging Face, 2019) went after "too heavy". It uses knowledge distillation: a small student model learns to copy a large teacher model.

    From BERT-Base to DistilBERT, as the video summarises it

    The paper says "60% faster", which is the same thing as 1.6x. And notice the last row: DistilBERT dropped NSP before RoBERTa showed that was safe.

    Where distillation comes from. The idea was popularised by Geoffrey Hinton's 2014 talk "Dark knowledge", and the paper "Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network" (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015). The video quotes it:

    "The soft targets contain almost all the knowledge."

    Geoffrey Hinton

    The setup. The same input x goes into both the teacher T and the student S. The teacher produces a probability distribution ŷT, the student produces ŷS, and the loss 𝓛 measures how different they are. The student is trained to make its distribution match the teacher's.

    Why copy a teacher at all, instead of learning straight from the labels? Because the teacher gives soft answers, and soft answers carry extra information. Take the sentence "The movie was fine." Its true label is neutral.

    The soft label tells the student something the hard label cannot: this sentence is closer to positive than to negative. The student learns how the teacher "thinks", not just its final answer. That extra information hidden in the small probabilities is Hinton's "dark knowledge".

    The distillation loss

    To train the student, you need one number that says how far its distribution is from the teacher's. That number is the KL divergence (Kullback-Leibler divergence), a standard way to measure how different two probability distributions are:

    KL(ŷ_T || ŷ_S) = Σ_i ŷ_T(i) · log( ŷ_T(i) / ŷ_S(i) )
    SymbolMeaning
    ieach possible class (or each word in the vocabulary)
    ŷ_T(i)the teacher's probability for class i
    ŷ_S(i)the student's probability for class i
    lognatural logarithm

    How to read it. For each class, compare the two probabilities with a ratio. If they are equal, the ratio is 1, log(1) = 0, and that class adds nothing. The bigger the mismatch, the bigger the loss. Each term is weighted by the teacher's probability, so the classes the teacher cares about most count the most. KL is always 0 or positive, and exactly 0 only when the two distributions match.

    Distillation is still used heavily today to make small, fast versions of big LLMs.

    ALBERT, ELECTRA and DeBERTa

    Three more models each picked one of BERT's weak spots and fixed it in a different way.

    ALBERT: fewer parameters A Lite BERT, Lan et al., Google, 2019

    ALBERT cut the parameter count with two tricks:

    1. Factorized embeddings. Instead of one 30,000 × 768 embedding table, use a 30,000 × 128 table, then a 128 × 768 projection up to the model's width.
    2. Cross-layer parameter sharing. All 12 layers use the same weights.
    BERT: 30,000 × 768 ALBERT: 30,000 × 128 then 128 × 768

    It also replaced NSP with SOP (Sentence Order Prediction): are these two sentences in the right order, or swapped? That is a harder, more useful task than NSP. ALBERT-base has about 12 million parameters against BERT-base's 110 million. The catch: it is smaller in memory but not faster, because the same layer still runs 12 times.

    ELECTRA: learning from every token Clark et al., Stanford and Google, 2020

    BERT only learns from the 15% of tokens it masks. ELECTRA fixed that. A small generator fills masked words with plausible replacements, and the main model, the discriminator, looks at every token and decides: original or replaced?

    This is called replaced token detection. Every token now gives a training signal, not just 15%, and ELECTRA matched RoBERTa's performance using about a quarter of the compute.

    DeBERTa: separating content from position He et al., Microsoft, 2020

    DeBERTa (Decoding-enhanced BERT with disentangled attention) connects straight back to Part 2. Instead of adding position into the word embedding, the problem we noted with learned and sinusoidal methods, DeBERTa keeps content and relative position as separate vectors. Attention is then computed from three terms: content-to-content, content-to-position and position-to-content.

    It was among the first models to beat human baseline performance on the SuperGLUE benchmark, and it is still a strong choice for classification.

    What actually makes these models smarter

    The architecture changes above make models more efficient and more stable, and that is what lets them be trained bigger and for longer. But the intelligence itself mostly comes from four things.

    1. Scale

      More parameters, more data, more compute. Research on scaling laws (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022, the "Chinchilla" paper) showed that performance improves predictably as these grow, and that data should grow along with model size. This is RoBERTa's lesson taken to the extreme. It also connects back to the 6ND formula from Lecture 1:

      compute = 6 × N × D (N = parameters, D = training tokens)

    2. Better data

      Filtering, deduplication, more code and maths, and carefully mixed sources. Data quality often matters more than a clever layer.

    3. Post-training

      A pretrained model only predicts the next word. Turning it into a helpful assistant takes further stages:

      • SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning): training on examples of good responses.
      • RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) and related preference methods: training the model towards responses people rate as better.
      • Reasoning training: teaching the model to work through a problem step by step before it answers.

    4. Long context and efficient inference

      RoPE scaling, GQA, FlashAttention and sliding windows let models read whole codebases or books, which makes them useful for far more real tasks. Those are Part 2, Part 7, Part 5 and Part 6 of this lecture, doing real work.

    Where each type of model is most useful

    Decoder-only models get the headlines, but they are not the right tool for every job. Here is a practical guide.

    NeedBest fitWhy

    A very common real-world setup uses both families at once: an encoder retrieves the right documents, and a decoder LLM reads them and writes the answer. That is RAG.

    The whole story in one paragraph

    The 2017 transformer replaced recurrence with attention so training could run in parallel, but that meant the model lost its sense of order. Positional encoding put order back, evolving from added vectors (learned, sinusoidal) to relative methods built into attention (T5 bias, ALiBi) and finally to RoPE's rotations. Residual connections and normalization made deep stacks trainable, with pre-norm and RMSNorm making them more stable and cheaper. The feedforward network became the model's main store of knowledge and evolved into SwiGLU and Mixture of Experts. Attention's quadratic cost pushed researchers toward FlashAttention and sparse or local attention, and the memory cost of generation pushed them from MHA to GQA. Out of the original encoder-decoder design, three families emerged; BERT showed the power of bidirectional pretraining, RoBERTa showed that training recipe and scale matter enormously, and decoder-only models rode that lesson all the way to today's LLMs.
