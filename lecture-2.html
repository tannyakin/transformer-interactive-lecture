    From attention to modern LLMs

    Lecture 1 ended on one uncomfortable fact: attention has no idea what order the words are in. This lecture fixes that, fills in the rest of the transformer block, works out what it all costs to run, and follows the story from the 2017 paper to the models people use today. Every calculation plays out step by step, so keep a calculator nearby and check each number as it appears.

    Part 1: Why order disappears

    Attention compares every word with every other word. So why can't it tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog"?

    An RNN never had this problem, because reading one word at a time is itself a sense of order. The transformer gave that up for speed, so it has to put order back some other way. That other way is positional encoding.

    Part 5: What attention costs

    Why does doubling the length of a text make attention four times as expensive?

    FlashAttention makes attention much faster in practice, but it still does n² work. To actually cut the n², you have to stop computing every pair. That brings us to sparse attention.

    Sparse attention cuts the cost of computing scores. But another cost only shows up when a model generates text, and it changed how attention heads themselves are designed.

    Part 8: Three model families

    Encoder, decoder or both: which one do you need, and why did decoder-only win?

    Encoder-only models did not disappear. They are still the go-to for embeddings, search, classification and RAG, because they are small, fast and cheap. Which brings us to the most important encoder model of all.

    How did one encoder, trained by filling in blanks, change the whole field?

    BERT had clear weak spots: it was heavy for its time, it learned from only 15% of its tokens, and it was undertrained. Each model that came next went after one of them.
